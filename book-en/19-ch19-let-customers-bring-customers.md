# Chapter 19 Let Customers Bring Customers: Word of Mouth, Referrals, and Virality

Once the contract is signed and the delivery is done, most people's habit is to treat the deal as finished—the bit of trust the customer has built up just sits there, unused.

The next customer often arrives because someone brought them over, and that is trust the customer produced themselves. So this chapter gives you 5 methods to turn passive waiting into active triggering.

---

## Method 1: Ask for the referral at the customer's happiest moment

**What to do**: Don't casually drop "have a friend introduce me" when the contract is signed—the customer hasn't gotten results yet, and the more warmly you ask, the more politely he declines. Wait until he has used the results and is at his emotional peak, then get the referral out with one sentence.

**How**

1. First define what "the happiest moment" looks like in your business: the project passes acceptance, the first renewal, when he has just reported your results to his boss, when a failure was resolved by you within two hours. Industries differ, but it must map to a concrete action, not a vague feeling like "great working together."
2. Write this moment into the delivery process, and whoever owns that step does the asking: the project manager asks at project delivery, customer service asks when resolving a support issue. Don't save it all up until year-end and have sales send out one mass "please refer us."
3. Ask for only one action. In that single sentence, ask the customer for only one thing—"if anyone you know runs into the same problem, just pass my name along."
4. Give the customer a concrete person to think of. Ask "do you have a friend who needs this?" and most people can't think of anyone on the spot; ask "in your industry, aren't there a few companies dealing with the same thing?" and his brain starts naming them.
5. Any customer you haven't asked within a month goes back on the list and into the next trigger point. Referrals aren't a once-and-done ask; they're three or four asks a year.
6. Let the person who asked know the result. If the customer really refers someone and that person closes, send the news back to the project manager or support agent who asked. If the asker gets no feedback, the second ask is just going through the motions.

**Example**: Pinduoduo (Chinese: 拼多多) welded "invite friends to join a group buy" into the purchase-completion page—tap "pay now" to reach the completion page, tap invite, and a chat list pops up, merging purchase and sharing into a single action; based on its prospectus, 2017 customer acquisition cost was about 11 yuan per person [third-party verified: quoting the prospectus].

**Pitfalls**: Treating "asking for referrals" as a sales target pushed down onto sales, forcing them to ask everyone they meet. Once a customer feels he is being sold on a network of relationships, he closes up immediately; ask that one sentence at the wrong moment and you lose every opportunity after it.

## Method 2: Write the referral copy so all the customer has to do is forward it

**What to do**: Customers are willing to forward for you; they are not willing to write the words for you. You write the passage first, so all they have to do is copy, paste, and send—under ten seconds start to finish. Finding the words looks trivial, but it is the main reason most referrals stall at "I'll ask around for you sometime."

**How**

1. Prepare three lengths: a one-sentence version (for a WeChat group), a three-sentence version (for Moments or a friend's DM), and a one-page version (for a decision maker who has to pass it up the chain). Keep all three in the same place, so the customer can take whichever one he needs.
2. Every piece of copy must include: who the customer is, what problem he ran into, what you did, what the result was, and what kind of person you are looking for. For the result, put in a checkable number or fact wherever possible—only when the customer can state the details will the listener take it seriously.
3. Put no exaggerated words and no sensitive customer information in the copy. If you need to use the customer's name or data, ask first whether you can reveal it and to what extent.
4. Make a forwarding card: a block of text plus a link or QR code, so the customer doesn't have to dig through your website for an entry point.
5. Leave the customer room to say "you can change this directly." The copy is a template; the customer changes a couple of words into his own voice, and only then does it sound like something he said rather than you reading a script behind him.
6. Refresh the copy once a quarter. Forward the same passage for too long and the customer himself starts to feel he is doing advertising on someone's behalf.

**Example**: Duolingo has offered a figure: about 80% of acquisition comes from word of mouth, from viral distribution, which keeps its customer acquisition cost very low [first-hand interview: HBR podcast]. Getting the customer to do nothing but forward is the first half of that mechanism.

**Pitfalls**: Writing the copy as a formal letter of recommendation. The longer and more formal the script, the less the customer wants to send it—what he wants is a passage he can paste straight into the chat box.

## Method 3: Separate cash-back for buying reviews from rewards for referring new customers

**What to do**: Treat "spending money to buy reviews" and "spending money to buy new customers" as two separate things. The former is a practice the enforcement authorities have called out by name; the latter is the normal incentive for referrals. Mix the two into one campaign plan and when trouble comes you won't be able to tell which half crossed the line.

**How**

1. First shut down every incentive in the company with the word "review" attached: cash-back, rebates, free samples, subsidies for posting order screenshots, points for a five-star review—if what it buys is that review, it counts.
2. Write a separate rule for referral rewards; what it rewards is the act of "bringing in a real customer." What is rewarded, how much, and when it is paid out—write it into one document that both sales and support follow.
3. Don't tie the reward to "review content." What you give the referrer is a reward for "you brought in a new customer," not attached to a condition like "and please write us a good review while you're at it."
4. Keep asking for customer reviews as usual, just on a separate track: after delivery, ask the customer to speak honestly, with no reward, no filtering of content, and keep the bad reviews too.
5. File the reward disbursement records, the campaign copy, and the customer communications. If you are ever asked, having material that shows clearly "why this money was paid" beats trying to recall it after the fact.
6. To check the text of the statutes, go to the National Laws and Regulations Database (Chinese: 国家法律法规数据库) at flk.npc.gov.cn, search the regulation name on the site, and locate the specific clause page before reading.

**Example**: The legal-education article from the Quanzhou Municipal Administration for Market Regulation (Chinese: 泉州市市场监督管理局) has a blunt headline—"It's clear now! 'Cash-back for good reviews' is illegal! Fines up to 2 million yuan" [official]. This guidance covers the practice of buying reviews with cash-back; it is a different matter from rewarding old customers for bringing in new ones [our judgment].

**Pitfalls**: Thinking "as long as the reward is worded vaguely, I'm fine." Customers claiming a reward already feel the money is owed to them; vague wording only adds a round of trust erosion between you and them. Worse, when the two kinds of money are paid out in one plan, even you can't say afterward which is which.

## Method 4: Log every referral as a traceable chain

**What to do**: Give each existing customer a tagged referral entry point, so "who brought whom" stays in the system rather than depending on the customer mentioning it in a chat. Once the chain is connected, you know whom to reward, who is worth asking again, and which customer's referrals actually close.

**How**

1. Give each existing customer a dedicated QR code or link; when he sends it out, anyone who clicks through carries the source automatically.
2. On the new customer's first contact, add one question—"who referred you?"—and record that answer as a field, not just in the sales rep's chat history.
3. After a deal closes, update the status back on the referrer: how many people he brought, how many closed, whether the reward was paid. Review this table once a quarter.
4. Calculate the conversion rate of referred leads separately, apart from other channels. Mix them together and you neither know whether referrals work nor whom to reward.
5. Small teams without a system start with a spreadsheet: referrer, referred person, contact time, progress, deal amount, reward status—six columns is enough.
6. Once a quarter, look back at who the top few "bringers" are and single them out. These are the relationships you most need to maintain, worth a clearer rule of their own.

**Example**: HONOR (Chinese: 荣耀) hard-coded attribution—"when several sharers invite the same friend, the system rewards only the sharer who last clicked the link, and only once"—and made clear it collects the sharer's UID and the order number and model of the referred person [company official campaign rules]; Amazon Associates, by contrast, uses "one Tracking ID per channel" to keep the stats separate [official help center].

**Pitfalls**: Learning who referred someone only when someone asks. Break the chain and you reward from impression alone; the customer will soon notice that these rewards are handed out at random.

## Method 5: Don't trade short-term volume for incentivized-sharing gimmicks

**What to do**: Delete the "forward to win, collect likes to unlock, no sharing no prize" style of gimmick from your acquisition playbook. The volume it buys doesn't stick, and it also sits right on the platforms' red lines; if you do the math on cost, what you lose isn't just that giveaway budget.

**How**

1. Go through your existing campaigns and mark anything that says "must forward to claim," "share to N groups to unlock," or "only counts once you have enough likes" as this category.
2. Think through what these campaigns actually bring: mostly accounts that showed up for the freebie, not customers. Such accounts never close later, and they drag down the conversion figures for your whole lead pipeline.
3. For the reach you actually need, switch to "the content itself is worth forwarding": a set of data your peers don't have, a template that can be used directly, a public talk that is genuinely clear.
4. Before a campaign goes live, run it against the platform's rules first—don't wait to be actioned before going back to read them.
5. Keep an internal list of campaigns the platforms have actioned, noting exactly which sentence violated which rule. Check that list before the next campaign; it's less work than memorizing the rules.

**Example**: WeChat's "Regulations on the Management of External Link Content" spells out incentivized sharing clearly—links of this kind are subject to action inside WeChat [official]. Checking it clause by clause before a campaign is far cheaper than explaining afterward. **The rules are written by others, and changes to them won't notify you first**—taking the "must share" action out of the campaign is the only way not to gamble on the rules. [our judgment]

**Pitfalls**: Confusing "the customer is willing to forward on his own" with "incentivized sharing" in the rules, so you become afraid to do even normal content distribution. What the rules target is that forced "must share" action.

## Notes for this chapter

- This chapter does not give industry figures for referral rates or viral coefficients. The source material has no comparable data whose provenance can be verified, and putting numbers in could only be fabrication. [our judgment]
- The current version of the Anti-Unfair Competition Law was not located down to the clause page, so this chapter cites no specific article numbers. To check the statutory text, search the regulation name on the National Laws and Regulations Database (Chinese: 国家法律法规数据库) at flk.npc.gov.cn.
- No authoritative comparison explaining the difference between "cash-back for good reviews" and "reward for forwarding" was collected. Method 3 separates "buying reviews" from "rewarding referred new customers" on the basis of the enforcement authorities' public legal-education position that names the former, and is our judgment.
- The FTC guide is a US official position, covering review and testimonial disclosure in the US market. Whether it can be applied directly to your Chinese-language business depends on where you sell.
- This chapter contains no effectiveness multiples.
