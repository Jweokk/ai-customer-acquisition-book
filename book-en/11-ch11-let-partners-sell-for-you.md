# Chapter 11: Letting Others Sell for You: Channels, Resellers, and Ecosystem Directories

In the old playbook, channels were built by people on the road: find resellers, negotiate distribution, work the trade shows exchanging business cards, grinding out one partner at a time. Now there is an additional class of ready-made positions — a platform's cloud marketplace, app directory, service-provider listing — that customers go into and browse on their own, and whether your thing is on the shelf decides whether a partner can sell you along the way.

Customers are often brought in by partners. So this chapter gives you 5 methods to treat channels and the service-provider directories of AI platforms as acquisition positions.

---

## Method 1: Treat partner directories as acquisition positions, not as sales channels

**What to do**: Establish first whether your product has a page in the platform directory that can be found by search, and only then talk about whether a partner will sell for you.

**How**

1. Search the cloud marketplace and app marketplace using the customer's own words: search the category term, your product name, your competitor's product name. Record three things — whether you are in the results, what position you are in, and who is ahead of you.
2. Separate two things: **the page in the directory** is your gateway, and **the partner's selling actions** are the channel. With no gateway built, a channel however wide is useless. Many companies sign a reseller first and only then discover that customers cannot find them in the platform at all.
3. Treat onboarding as "putting up a page": the page has to be written and maintained by you. A resale contract can wait once it is signed, but a page left unattended will produce nothing at all.
4. Once the page is built, give the address to your existing partners. When they make a quote or send a proposal, they attach the product-page link directly; the customer who clicks through sees the platform's domain and transaction flow, and it gets forwarded more readily than a PDF you made yourself [our judgment].
5. Search again quarterly and record the position in the same table. Directory categories and ordering get adjusted, and searching once a year shows you no change.

**Example**: Shopify's app store is an acquisition position rather than a sales channel — more than 10,000 apps, 561 million USD paid to partners each year, and 85% of merchants depend on at least one app to run their business [third-party verified]. Here partners are traffic, not channels.

**Pitfalls**: Thinking that onboarding means someone will sell for you. The platform gives you only a shelf slot and a backend; after listing, nobody maintains the page for you and nobody answers customers for you.

## Method 2: Read the commission and liability clauses verbatim before you sign

**What to do**: Review the platform's onboarding agreement as a contract about to be signed, focusing on four things — who sets the price, who charges, who bears liability, and how to exit.

**How**

1. Start with the money clauses: who sets the platform service fee, how it is charged, and whether it can be changed. Do not get one line from your account contact on "roughly what the cut is" and move on — that sentence does not amend the agreement.
2. Then look at pricing authority: can you set the product price yourself, and has the platform reserved the power of "listed only after approval"?
3. Then look at liability: for customer complaints, refunds, and disputes, who takes them first and who ultimately covers them. This clause decides how many people you keep in after-sales.
4. Finally look at exit: how the agreement renews at expiry, whether the platform can terminate unilaterally, and who handles after-sale service for goods already sold once it is terminated.
5. Copy these clauses onto one page and hand it to the boss who pays and signs. Channel terms are business terms and they touch cash flow and liability; they should not be decided by the business department alone.
6. Gather your materials in advance: corporate real-name verification, legal-representative ID, corporate qualification certificates, and any industry license the category requires. The agreement states that for categories requiring a license or approval to operate, the service provider itself is responsible for obtaining the license and for proactively submitting it to the platform for review [official] — get stuck at this step and everything discussed earlier comes to nothing.

**Example**: The text of Alibaba Cloud's (Chinese: 阿里云) Cloud Marketplace Service Provider Onboarding Agreement states these things: the platform service fee is collected by the operator, the fee standard is decided by the operator and may also be modified; the service provider has the right to define and price the goods in its store independently, but goods must pass a security review before publishing, with the operator reserving the decision on whether to list them; the service provider signs an independent goods agreement with users in its own name, and seller liability is borne independently by the service provider; users may not be steered to transact outside the cloud marketplace without the platform's written permission; one service provider is allowed only one store in the cloud marketplace; and the operator has the right to terminate the agreement on 15 days' written notice [official].

**Pitfalls**: Negotiating only the rebate and never reading the liability clauses. Then the first customer dispute arrives, and you discover the agreement says the platform bears no liability for the goods — while you had already staffed up on the assumption that "the platform will cover it."

## Method 3: Write the directory product page as a set of search fields

**What to do**: Fill in the product name, category, details, and the service standards you promise entirely according to what customers will search.

**How**

1. Get the category right. The category decides which shelf you sit on; pick the wrong one and you are in someone else's showroom.
2. Write what problem you solve first, then the specifications. A customer browsing the directory arrives with one thing in mind; specs are step two.
3. Read the platform's entry threshold carefully. Some marketplaces require the product to run on their own resources, or to be bundled with their own products, or to serve as a companion tool — fall short of the threshold and listing is not allowed, and at that point no description, however good, helps.
4. Write your delivery and service commitments into an external document and hang it next to the product: delivery method, billing method, support hours, response time. This document is the only evidence a customer can look at before ordering.
5. Platform product pages are often distributed to affiliated sites. Write on the assumption that the reader cannot see your own website, so that the key information closes the loop within the page itself.

**Example**: The Alibaba Cloud Marketplace agreement sets a hard listing threshold — the product must run on Alibaba Cloud resources, or be sold bundled with Alibaba Cloud products, or be a companion tool used closely with Alibaba Cloud products; otherwise listing is not permitted [official]. The same agreement also authorizes the platform to list a service provider's goods on affiliated platforms such as Bailian (Chinese: 百炼) and Qwen Cloud (Chinese: 千问云) [official]. The agreement also sets service standards the goods must meet: non-onsite technical support from 9:00 to 18:00 every business day, a solution or alternative method within 24 hours when the software cannot run and the user cannot use it, and a solution within 48 hours when a performance decline makes it difficult to use [official].

**Pitfalls**: Writing the product page as an internal product manual. Thirty features are listed and not one of the questions the customer cares most about is answered.

## Method 4: Give partners a kit they can resell from directly

**What to do**: Build a partner kit so that a salesperson who has never seen your product can open their mouth to quote and answer objections after reading it.

**How**

1. A one-page resale pitch: who the customer is, what it solves, and where it differs from similar products. Do not write words like "empower" or "closed loop" — a partner reading them aloud to a customer will stumble.
2. Keep only two tiers in the price sheet: one public price and one partner price, with the validity period and whether it can be changed stated clearly. With too many tiers, the partner hesitates in front of the customer.
3. Three citable cases, each stating clearly the customer type, what was used, and how the result was calculated.
4. A one-page objection response: put in the questions customers ask most often along with the answers, including "what are your pitfalls?"
5. A one-page red list: what may not be said, what results may not be promised, and whether AI-generated assets need labeling. This page draws the line for partners, and the liability when something goes wrong lands on you.
6. Update once a year and note the change in the footer. Outdated material in a partner's hands is more trouble than no material in yours.

**Example**: The Alibaba Cloud Marketplace agreement requires service providers to give users necessary after-sales service and technical support, and turns support hours, fault-response times, and refund conditions into hard items — non-onsite support (phone, email, online) from 9:00 to 18:00 every business day, a solution within 24 hours when the software cannot run and the user cannot use it, a solution within 48 hours when a performance decline makes it difficult to use, and a full refund before the transaction completes if the user is not satisfied (except for tool-type goods) [official]. These items can be copied directly into the service-commitment page of a partner kit: what the platform requires you to do is exactly what the customer will eventually come back and hold you to.

**Pitfalls**: Handing over one product deck and nothing else. The partner still does not know what price to quote, and cannot field "can you do better on price?"

## Method 5: Never let one channel stand alone; run a dependency check every quarter

**What to do**: Work out the contribution of each directory and each reseller, so you can see plainly which channel has grown too large for you to withdraw from.

**How**

1. Build a channel table with fixed fields: channel name, type (platform directory / reseller / distributor), go-live date, leads this quarter, closed deals, platform service fee or discount, and share of leads.
2. Look at the directory type and the reseller type separately. The directory type is self-service — you owe no favors but must maintain the page; the reseller type needs a person following it, and when the person changes, the channel snaps.
3. Set your own ceiling on any single channel's share of leads. By this book's line, once any one channel is long-term eating more than half the lead volume, you should be preparing a second [our judgment].
4. Review the platform's rules page quarterly: whether the agreement has changed, whether the fee rate has moved, and whether there are new removal conditions. Once the agreement changes, it runs by the agreement, not by your say-so.
5. Set different targets for the two types. The directory type is judged on page impressions and self-service conversions; the reseller type on quote count and sales-cycle length. Compare them on the same measure and you will reach the wrong conclusion that "resellers are useless" or "directories are useless."
6. For the things that can be moved — lists, quotes, after-sales records — keep a copy in your own system. The data in the backend is not in your hands.

**Example**: The Alibaba Cloud Marketplace onboarding agreement states that platform rules take effect automatically on being posted on the website and become part of the agreement; the operator has the right to terminate on 15 days' written notice; and the agreement terminates when it expires without renewal, when the service provider triggers removal conditions, and in other such situations [official]. A channel position is rented under an agreement, and both the term and the rent are set by the platform.

**Pitfalls**: Writing "we got into a big vendor's marketplace" into your fundraising materials as a moat. Change the rules, terminate the agreement, and the page and the leads disappear together.

## Notes for this chapter

- "How many new customers a partner brings in" — this kind of channel-contribution ratio is a widely circulated industry saying, and this round found no original publisher. This chapter writes no channel-contribution ratio, and it advises readers to find the source themselves before citing one.
- Those two OpenAI official announcements ("Introducing apps in ChatGPT" and "Developers can now submit apps to ChatGPT") are direct material for "AI-platform app directories are turning into gateways," but this round's fetch returned 403 and the body text was not verified. This chapter therefore writes none of the content of those two announcements, only noting that they exist and that the direction points this way.
- This chapter has only two verifiably reachable sources, both of them Alibaba Cloud Marketplace's own rules pages. This is the platform's one-sided account; it does not represent other platforms or a common industry standard, and no conclusion can be drawn from it that "meeting these conditions gets you into any marketplace."
- The official listing requirements for Salesforce AppExchange, AWS Partner Network, and the Microsoft marketplace, and the official onboarding pages for domestic agent stores (Coze (Chinese: 扣子), Wenxin (Chinese: 文心), Yuanqi (Chinese: 元器), and the DingTalk (Chinese: 钉钉) and Feishu (Chinese: 飞书) app marketplaces), were not captured this round, so this chapter gives no onboarding conditions for these platforms.
- For the industry-standard channel-commission figures and the various "channel contribution share" statements, this round found not one original publisher, and no ratio appears in this chapter. The Alibaba Cloud Marketplace agreement only states that the operator has the right to collect a platform service fee and decide the fee standard; it gives no rate, and the body does not add one.
