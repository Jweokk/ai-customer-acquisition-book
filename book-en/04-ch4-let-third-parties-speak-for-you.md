# Chapter 4: Let Third Parties Speak for You

Third-party endorsements used to travel person to person and through press clippings — whoever placed more articles looked more credible; now buyers also ask AI the question "whom should I trust?", and the model only repeats what it has read elsewhere, no matter how well your own website is written. In 6sense's survey of nearly 4,000 B2B buyers, in 95% of cases the vendor that ultimately won the deal had already appeared on the buyer's "day-one candidate list" before the buyer first contacted sales [vendor claim]. What you need to do is shift your effort from "saying it yourself" to "getting others to say it," and cultivate the statements you yourself cannot call the shots on.

What buyers see at the AI is not what you say but how others evaluate you. So this chapter gives you 6 methods for cultivating the evidence that lives "on other people's turf."

---

## Method 1: Map who speaks for you and who gets you wrong

**What to do**: Build a table of the places that "speak for you" and the places that "get you wrong," split into three categories, and keep it updated.

**How**

1. Sweep review sites, rankings, industry media, community posts, podcasts, and wiki-type pages, and sort every entry into just three categories: mentioned and accurate, mentioned but inaccurate, not mentioned. The third category is where your next investment should go.
2. Take 6–10 real procurement questions — the ones your salespeople are asked over and over on calls — and ask each of them several times across 5 AI channels, noting the context in which you are mentioned. The point is to record **who is being cited**, not only whether you show up; the sources being cited are the ones you need to court.
3. Repeat every 30 days, watching four numbers: the number of citations, your standing in third-party rankings and reviews, the accuracy rate of what gets relayed about you, and the number of sessions AI refers to you.
4. Remember that this kind of evidence is not meant to move people; it is meant to save the buyer one round of personal verification. Buyers were never going to compare exhaustively, and the size of the candidate set is the optimum they calculate between "decision cost" and "the marginal benefit of shopping around more"; an independent review or a ranking position saves them one verification step [third-party verified].
5. Read the same table again through the lens of how the buying group divides its work. Finance wants to know the overrun boundary, IT wants to know integration, legal wants to know the terms, and the business side wants to know whether anyone actually uses it — a Gartner survey of 632 B2B buyers in May 2025 found that 74% of buying teams experience "unhealthy conflict" during the decision process, while customization aimed only at individual-level relevance has a 59% negative impact on the buying group's ability to reach consensus [vendor claim].

**Example**: In Ahrefs' study of 75,000 brands, the correlation between brand mentions and AI visibility was about 3 times that of traditional backlinks (on the order of 0.664 versus 0.218), while the number of content pages was almost irrelevant (about 0.194) [vendor claim].

**Pitfalls**: Fixating on the single question of "did I show up?" What actually needs fixing is the "who is being cited" list, not the wording on your own website.

(Industry differences: SaaS looks at rankings and technical communities like G2 and TrustRadius; consumer and local life look at review sites and notes; manufacturing and industrial look at associations, trade shows, and technical communities — the gateways differ, the action is the same.)

## Method 2: Spend your PR budget on making material

**What to do**: Move money and people from "getting yourself to appear" to "making material that third parties can cite."

**How**

1. Split the money along a workable structure: core SEO 40%, digital PR a quarter, data and reports 20%, training 10%, experiments 5% [third-party verified]. The ratios need not be copied, but the "digital PR" line has to be recast as making material, not placing articles.
2. Switch the KPI track: article placement volume and clipping counts are void; look instead at "the number and context of independent third-party citations" and "your standing in third-party rankings and reviews."
3. Pick 3 high-authority third parties — one vertical review site, one industry publication, one video or podcast channel. Do just one thing for each: give it something it would want to cite and that only you can provide, such as an exclusive data set, a comparison checklist, an open-source tool, or a citable expert explanation.
4. Write the goal as "being cited," not "being published." Publication only proves you bought a slot; citation proves your material is useful.
5. Rewrite your internal fact list into a form a third party can copy directly: a one-sentence conclusion + the data + the time point + a verifiable link. A form anyone can copy away is a good form.
6. Before you do this, accept one comparison: among the consumer brands that rose on "cheap Meta ads + Shopify + subsidies," the ones that survived had one thing in common — they were mentioned most by third parties (review sites, editorial media, podcasts, creators); the ones that spent the most on acquisition did not survive [vendor claim].

**Example**: In its February 2024 S-1 filing, Reddit disclosed that the data licensing agreements signed in January 2024 were worth a total of $203 million with terms of 2–3 years, that it expected to recognize at least $66.4 million in revenue that year, and its risk factors stated that substantially all of the contract value came from a single partner [official].

**Pitfalls**: Judging source-building by click volume. In July 2025 Pew found that when an AI summary appears, only 1% of users click its citation links [third-party verified]; promising results on the basis of clicks is digging your own hole.

## Method 3: Get customers to write reviews for you on public platforms

**What to do**: Make "ask the customer to leave a review on a public platform" a fixed step after signing.

**How**

1. After signing, set one action: ask the customer to leave a public-platform review (G2, TrustRadius, Dianping (Chinese: 大众点评), Tmall (Chinese: 天猫) reviews, and the like) that includes a specific usage scenario — where it is used, what it solved, and what still is not good about it.
2. Three lines must not be crossed: real customers, real experience, no conditions traded. Do not buy positive reviews, do not hire paid posters, do not trade benefits for reviews — not even for the "right to leave a bad review." Platform rules likewise prohibit inducing shares through monetary rewards, lotteries, and the like.
3. Customers may give you a low score, but every entry must be verifiable. When you hand the product over for third-party review, hand over your scope and boundaries along with it — better a low score than an unverifiable sample.
4. You may publish rankings you made yourself, but you must disclose that you made them. Disguising them as an "independent review" is squarely illegal: on August 14, 2024, the FTC passed the Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule by a 5-0 vote, making it explicit for the first time that a company-controlled review site falsely claiming independence is unlawful [official].
5. The logos on your case-study wall must be verifiable too. A March 2025 TechCrunch investigation found that the customer logos displayed on the website of AI SDR company 11x were not real customers — two of them had run only about a one-month trial before stopping — and the report said its total customer retention rate was below 50% [third-party verified].
6. Every quarter, feed the recurring misunderstandings and boundaries in reviews back into the failure-conditions list and the pre-sales FAQ. Devices that "voluntarily make a commitment" — warranties, branding, licensing — are exactly the kind of thing the market grew on its own to counter information asymmetry, and public customer reviews belong to the same family [third-party verified].

**Example**: In the TruHeight case, the FTC alleged that thousands of five-star reviews on its website were written by company employees, that five-star reviews were exchanged for free or discounted products, and that software-generated reviews were posted from bot accounts disguised as real users; it ended in a $4 million judgment [official].

**Pitfalls**: Buying reviews as advertising material. Among the typical cases announced after China's Interim Provisions on Anti-Unfair Competition in Cyberspace in May 2024, a company in Hangzhou provided "shoot A, ship B" order-brushing services for 589,000 orders and was fined 725,000 yuan; a club paid 560 yuan to hire people to fabricate 8 fake negative reviews slandering a competitor and was fined 10,000 yuan [official: official-media relay].

## Method 4: Put the founder front and center, and say unfavorable things under a real name

**What to do**: Have the founder or a frontline expert speak under his or her own name — about the situation, about losses, about "this kind of customer is not a fit for us."

**How**

1. Pick the person: real name, first person. The content team only tidies up; it does not ghostwrite — once ghostwriting becomes widespread, the cost difference disappears and the whole action becomes pointless.
2. Pick the subject matter: losses, failure cases, unfavorable data, experiences of being demoted by a platform. Competitors can copy your opinions, but not your situation.
3. Pick the frequency: at least one piece a month, signed, with verifiable numbers, and with primary materials attached wherever possible.
4. Unfavorable statements must come with judgment criteria and remedial actions. Laying out only the problems without giving what to do is self-exposure.
5. Use trust data to settle internal arguments so you do not have to debate aloud round after round: Edelman's 2025 Trust Barometer Special Report on Brands lists "brands must move from SEO to GEO" as the first of its ten findings, and states that what appears in AI is determined by reputation, relevance, credibility, and clarity, and driven by earned (word-of-mouth and third-party endorsement); the same-period figure is that 80% of people trust the brands they use, and 91% of people who use generative AI platforms use them in some way for shopping research [official: institutional report].
6. Do not turn "human touch" into a template. Signaling economics says it plainly: for a signal to be credible, the cost of obtaining it must differ enough between senders; if everyone's cost is the same, it carries no information [third-party verified].

**Example**: Scott Lapatine, founder of the independent music publication Stereogum, reported in the first person in a relaunch announcement that Google's shift toward AI search cut their ad revenue by 70%, and at the same time declared "we do not use generative AI" [official].

**Pitfalls**: Thinking that "coming across as sincere" is enough. In SurveyMonkey's survey of 2,017 U.S. adults, 79% strongly preferred human service and only 8% clearly preferred AI, while 14% would stop trusting a company because "the AI they interacted with did not disclose its identity" [vendor claim] — buyers trust a statement that can be traced back to a specific person.

## Method 5: In communities, answer questions first — do not lead with your product

**What to do**: Make community answers a measured, fixed practice, and build believability in places where the rules are not yours to write.

**How**

1. Choose 1–2 real people (an engineer or a product lead) to answer questions on Reddit, Zhihu (Chinese: 知乎), industry forums, and Discord under their real names and company identities, without slipping in links or promotions.
2. Give before you take: answering one specific question is closer to external evidence than posting one product introduction.
3. Disclose your identity: who is speaking, whom they represent, and what relationship they have to the brand — all stated plainly.
4. Set a rhythm, and a ledger: answer a few questions on a fixed weekly schedule, and record what the question was, where it came from, who answered it, and whether it was cited.
5. The "real-person IP" in a private domain can only be run by real people. The moment it is switched to batch automated scripts, the community turns from an asset into a liability.

**Example**: In Greenway's 800-account controlled experiment, fully autonomous AI booked the most meetings, but the meeting-to-opportunity conversion rate was only 8%, versus 31% for human sales and 26% for a human-machine mix; aggressive sending knocked the primary domain's reputation from good to suspicious in 19 days, and recovery took 8 weeks [vendor claim].

**Pitfalls**: Treating "bulk accounts + soft articles + bots" as reach. In the short term it looks like reach; in reality it is overdrawing believability, and the recovery period is long.

## Method 6: Review every month how AI describes you, and fix the source when it is wrong

**What to do**: Build a monitoring-and-correction process for "being described wrongly," and push errors back to the source to fix.

**How**

1. Review AI's restatement of you every month, focusing on three places: price, functionality, and scope of applicability. When you find something attributed to the wrong thing, log it — which sentence, which platform, what date.
2. For corrections, use third-party-verifiable material to change the source; do not argue on your own website. A rebuttal on your own site is something you said yourself, and both the model and the buyer will discount it.
3. Distinguish three kinds of payoff: being cited is a brand payoff and can compound; being clicked is a traffic payoff and is declining; being trusted is a conversion payoff. The trust column is in fact the strongest — Adobe Analytics' March 2026 figures are that AI-referred visitors to U.S. retail sites converted 42% higher than non-AI visitors and stayed 45% longer [third-party verified: vendor-reported]. Mix them together in your accounting and you will certainly get it wrong.
4. Monitor what competitors do: buying ranking positions, inflating reviews, malicious comparison tests — keep evidence of all of it and go through the reporting channels.
5. Do not bet visibility on a single point. One platform changes its rules, or one crawler gets blocked, and you are cut off; two or more mutually independent source lines are what survive a single change. Content licensing and unauthorized crawling are being fought out in court, and platforms are installing valves — publishers that want to block AI crawlers often fall out of the search index at the same time; in China, gateway issues like "blocking web links" have been made a focus of special rectification [official].

**Example**: Digital Content Next's eight-week tracking of 19 members showed a median year-over-year decline of 10% in referral traffic from Google search; among the evidence submitted to UK regulators was also a case of "ranked first but click-through rate fell from 5.1% to 0.6%" [third-party verified].

**Pitfalls**: Using AI-referred traffic as the acceptance criterion for source-building. The click column is low to begin with, and AI-referred visitors are not of poor quality — one media outlet has self-reported that the subscription conversion rate of its AI-channel visitors was 4–5 times that of traditional search [third-party verified: media-reported].

## Notes for this chapter

- The Ahrefs, Peec, Leapd, 6sense, Gartner, Greenway, SurveyMonkey and other data cited in this chapter all come from vendors' or research firms' own work, whose methodology appendices are not public, so all count as [vendor claim]. They agree in direction (mentions beat backlinks, sources are highly concentrated) and can corroborate one another, but **the specific coefficients cannot be extrapolated into industry constants**.
- "Being cited by AI" and "getting traffic" are two separate sets of books. Third-party media tracking shows the citation table rising and the click table falling (DCN members' referral traffic median -10%, Stereogum's ad revenue -70%), so source-building buys eligibility and cannot be treated as a traffic promise.
- No verifiable public pricing case can currently be found for content licensing in the Chinese ecosystem; the only price reference in this chapter is Reddit's $203 million deal; domestically the issue is more one of gateway blocking and interconnection, and the two sides are not directly comparable.
- Third-party ranking positions and review placements can be bought or inflated, so you have to check the credibility of the rankings yourself. The FTC has already written "a company-controlled review site falsely claiming independence" into law as unlawful, and on the Chinese side order-brushing and hiring people to write negative reviews also already have penalty cases.
- The claim that the Google–Reddit licensing fee is "about $60 million a year" comes from a rumored figure relayed by the media; the S-1 does not name the amount or the counterparty, so this book does not adopt that number.
