# Chapter 18 Phone and Private Domains: What Still Works, and What Blows Up the Moment You Touch It

Running phone and private-domain work used to be a contest of whether you had enough people, whether the script was smooth enough, whether the list was big enough; now calls can be dialed automatically, messages sent automatically, scripts written automatically — and regulation and the platforms have, at just the same time, tightened these two openings: the positions you can touch have narrowed, and the cost of touching the wrong one has become very concrete, landing on your accounts, your lines, and your company. What you have to do is first draw the red lines clearly, then decide which segment of this work can be handed to machines.

At this step the customer is already in a one-to-one conversation, but regulators are watching here too. So this chapter gives you 5 methods to make the red lines clear and keep your accounts and lines alive.

---

## Method 1: Split this work into four segments, and give only the first to machines

**What to do**: Split "outreach" into four segments — who to find, what to say, after the other side speaks, and whether it's worth occupying a sales rep's time — and decide segment by segment who does it.

**How**

1. The first segment is the list and research: who to find, and why this person is relevant right now. This segment can be handed to AI — it deals with public information, creates no consent problem, and what it saves is computable work hours. The overseas figure is about $262 per lead for a human SDR and about $39 for AI [third-party verified]; on the Chinese side, the AI background checks in foreign-trade CRMs bring the cost per run down to 5% of the original [third-party verified].
2. The second segment is first touch and writing: what to say, and in whose name to say it. AI can only produce a draft; the right of attribution must stay in human hands.
3. The third segment is follow-up and reply: what happens after the other side speaks. Not one step in this segment may be led by a machine.
4. The fourth segment is the meeting and qualification: whether this person is worth occupying a sales rep's time is decided by a human; AI is only responsible for the pre-meeting homework.
5. The billing definition follows the segmentation. The research segment is usually billed as credits deducted per action — ten thousand more list entries burn ten thousand more actions, and failed retries are charged the same, with no refund [third-party verified]. "AI is cheap" doesn't hold in this part of the process.
6. Settle all four segments with the same ruler: meeting count is the vendor's metric; cost per qualified opportunity is your metric.

**Example**: In a controlled experiment with 800 accounts, two quarters, and four platforms, fully autonomous agents booked the most meetings, but the meeting-to-opportunity conversion rate was only 8%; human SDRs were 31%, and a hybrid approach with AI drafting and human approval was 26%. Reckoned as "cost per qualified opportunity," fully autonomous was $588 each, purely human $1,006 each, and hybrid $342 each [vendor claim]. The same material claims that a pipeline where AI does the research and a human does the approval can reach 3.2 times the qualified meetings per person [vendor claim].

**Pitfalls**: Treating "meeting count" as an assessment metric. The numbers will look good; the valuable opportunities won't rise with them.

## Method 2: Write "AI never takes the second sentence" into policy

**What to do**: Write into policy, in stone, that "AI writes only the first sentence; after a reply a human takes over," and turn automatic follow-up off by default.

**How**

1. Write one sentence into policy, in stone: any reply is taken over by a human; AI never replies to a prospect's response. The system's automatic follow-up should be off by default, not "optional."
2. Leave a human gate before the first touch goes out: a person spends three minutes going over job title, milestones, and company news. What this gate catches is factual error — in the experiment above, 12% of first-touch emails contained factual errors, and 58% were sent to accounts that didn't fit the target customer profile [vendor claim].
3. In situations involving people, machines never lead: any conversation the other side has already replied to; conversations in which the other side is in a negative emotional state, such as crises, complaints, unsubscribes, and churn win-backs; one-to-one content sent in the personal name of a founder or executive.
4. In situations involving commitments, machines never lead: any statement involving price, contract terms, delivery time, or payment terms; any evaluation, comparison, or review of competitors; first touches in regulated categories like healthcare, finance, law, insurance, and education.
5. Hand high-ticket accounts back to people. A reference dividing line: below $25,000 ticket size and a cycle shorter than 30 days can go fully autonomous, above $50,000 with more than 5 decision-makers use humans, and the middle goes hybrid [third-party verified].
6. Explain the reason for this rule to the team: once the other side responds, this thing turns from an outreach you initiated into a two-way conversation the buyer is engaged in — and what the machine wants (send more, get more replies, book more meetings) directly conflicts with what the buyer wants (be disturbed less, get problems solved quickly).

**Example**: In the implementation framework of the 800-account experiment above, one rule that is bolded on its own is "any reply is taken over by a human." A failure case in the same material is the exact opposite of this rule: the other side replied "we just renewed with a competitor through 2027," and the system sent another round of value statements plus a calendar link, and was forwarded by the other side as an example of spam [vendor claim].

**Pitfalls**: Shutting off only "send" and not "reply." A problem on the send side can still be audited; a problem on the reply side directly burns a relationship you already had in hand.

## Method 3: Call only people who have consented

**What to do**: Before you start dialing, accept the real state of the phone line today, call only people who have explicitly consented, and keep the consent as a searchable record.

**How**

1. First post the real state on the wall. On the overseas side, 90% of sales calls go straight to voicemail, and only 2% of cold calls earn a meeting; the cold-call response rate fell from 8.5% in 2018 to 4.2% in 2022 [vendor claim]. The Chinese side is more direct: a single machine can dial 800–1,500 calls a day, at a cost as low as a bit over one mao per minute [third-party verified].
2. Call only people who have consented. On the Chinese side, Article 24 of the Regulation on the Implementation of the Law on the Protection of the Rights and Interests of Consumers states that, without the consumer's consent, a business operator may not send commercial information or make commercial calls; this regulation took effect on July 1, 2024 [official]. On the U.S. side, the FCC ruled on February 8, 2024 that AI-generated voices are "artificial voices" within the meaning of the Telephone Consumer Protection Act — outbound calls using AI voices likewise require consent first [official].
3. Keep four kinds of record: consent records, number sources, outbound call times and frequency, and the handling result for requests like "don't call again." What enforcement wants is not your explanation, it's the record.
4. Set calling hours and the interval for reaching the same number. In a central-media investigative report, machines dial "regardless of hours or target, carpet-bombing style" [third-party verified]; you should do the opposite.
5. Reckon "can you call" and "is it worth calling" separately. The cost of a single touch approaching zero doesn't mean this line still has a trust balance; once the complaint rate rises, the connection rate of the whole line collapses together with it.
6. Before outsourcing or buying numbers, ask three things: where the numbers come from, how consent was obtained, and how complaints are handled.

**Example**: A July 2026 investigative report by China.com and other central media said that after upgraded large-model agents entered the outbound-call field, marketing calls in real estate, education, finance, and insurance increased noticeably; machines "blind-dial" indiscriminately and generate numbers automatically, so a user who never registered can be hit at random; the industry also commonly equips a "one-tap de-marking" capability, with frequency up to three times a day; when a user complains about a number, the system can't even find the outbound record [third-party verified]. In the same period, the number of Telephone Consumer Protection Act compliance enforcement cases in the U.S. doubled from about 1,200 to more than 2,400 [vendor claim].

**Pitfalls**: Treating "AI voice is cheap" as "you can call more." Penalties are counted per call — on the U.S. side the statutory damages are $500 per call per violation, up to triple, that is $1,500, for willful or knowing violations [official].

## Method 4: Build the private domain on three things — consent, frequency, labels

**What to do**: Make the private domain about "people who have already consented, outreach with a quota, layered by labels," not "fishing mobile numbers out of a database."

**How**

1. Start from consent. The entire legitimacy of the private domain comes from the other side actively adding your WeCom, following your official account, or leaving a mobile number — not from that purchased number database in your hands. The moment you cross from "people who have consented" to "numbers in the database," the applicable legal environment immediately switches to another set.
2. Make frequency the red line. Write "how often you can touch a person" into the product logic, not into employees' self-discipline. Mature private-domain solutions already have hard rules like "the same user can be reached at most once in 24 hours" [vendor claim].
3. Make labels the fuel. The efficiency of the private domain comes from automatic tagging, layering, and live-code routing, not from more flowery copy. What this segment wants is "let the right people see the right thing."
4. Keep "added a friend" and "still interacting" on separate books. Friend count is stock; whether there was interaction within two weeks is flow.
5. Unsubscribe, refusal, and blocking must take effect immediately and sync to all sending channels, not take effect in only one back end.
6. Don't trade incentives for forwarding. Article 2.1.2 of WeChat's Rules for the Management of External Link Content prohibits inducing sharing through benefits such as monetary rewards, lotteries, and task check-ins [official].

**Example**: Luckin Coffee (Chinese: 瑞幸咖啡) turned its private domain into account that can be computed — within three months of launching on WeCom, linked users grew from 1.8 million to nearly 20 million, and the automation rate of marketing rules exceeded 90% [first-person account: founder interview].

**Pitfalls**: Thinking that adding more friends equals doing the private domain well. Friend count keeps rising and interaction never moves — "add and go silent" is the endpoint of this road.

## Method 5: Give your accounts and lines a health-check sheet, and budget for bans

**What to do**: List the "rented assets" — domains, platform accounts, outbound numbers, WeCom accounts — as a health sheet, each item spelling out its health metric, warning line, owner, and recovery time.

**How**

1. First remember that a ban has three layers, each more expensive than the last: reputation bans on domains and senders, account bans at the platform level, and entity-level bans that reach the company itself. The first two make it so you "can't get in"; the third changes the nature of your business.
2. Write three fields for each asset: its current health metric, its warning line, and the owner's name. "Some department is responsible" turns into no one responsible at the first incident.
3. Write recovery time into the sheet too, because it determines whether you dare to try. After a domain's reputation is damaged, the recovery period disclosed in a controlled experiment is 8 weeks [vendor claim]; account bans are up to the platform to recover; after a number gets an effective complaint, the proper path is to change numbers.
4. Mark the "point of no return" for each item. Some losses can be repaired and some cannot: a domain can be warmed up, a reputation can be repaired, but a single effective complaint may render a number unusable for good, and "using technical means to bypass platform boundaries for customer acquisition" has already gone from gray to clearly unlawful.
5. Keep "can't get in" and "performs badly" on separate books. Poor performance can still be optimized; not being able to get in means you don't exist on the buyer's side — these two things need to be managed with different budget items.
6. Before buying outreach tools, ask three questions: where does the contact database come from, will it send on my primary domain or on a separate domain, and have any customer accounts on your side been banned in the last 24 months. If any answer is vague, stop first.

**Example**: Platform enforcement doesn't look at your funding round. Article 8.2 of the LinkedIn User Agreement explicitly prohibits using bots or other unauthorized automated means to send messages, download contacts, or scrape profiles [official]; at the end of 2025, Artisan, a star company in AI outreach, was banned by LinkedIn, and the CEO confirmed the ban to a tech outlet; after about two weeks of communication it was restored in January 2026, and during that time its sales lead pipeline was cut [third-party verified]. The Chinese side's actions were earlier and bigger: in April 2019 WeChat banned 30 million weishang marketing accounts [third-party verified]. The cost at the domain layer is equally concrete: a controlled experiment disclosed that an aggressive sending pattern knocked the primary domain's reputation from good to suspicious in 19 days, with recovery taking 8 weeks [vendor claim]. And the domain used for outreach is shared across the whole company — **composite profile｜constructed from a common industry phenomenon, not a named event**: once the reputation is damaged, even quotes to customers and confirmations to suppliers go into the spam folder too.

**Pitfalls**: Doing this sheet for the first time only after something has gone wrong. Asset wear shows up on the financial statements in the form of "performance getting worse"; by the time you see it, the moment when you could have stopped is usually already past.

## Notes for this chapter

- Every "how many times better" number in this chapter comes from vendor or service-provider self-reported retrospectives, without independent third-party verification. Cite them with their denominator attached; any multiple without a denominator should not be written into a budget sheet [our judgment].
- China's rules price by "consent," overseas rules price by "item." The Chinese side directly negates the entire category of "without consent" actions, so a mature approach shows up as list-source control and frequency control; overseas, an email and a call each carry their own price tag, so a mature approach shows up as delivery-infrastructure engineering. Using overseas domain-warming experience for Chinese outbound calling is useless, and moving China's private-domain labeling logic to overseas email doesn't connect either; the only three principles that travel between the two are: consent up front, traceability, and the ability to opt out [our judgment].
- The response rates for cold email and cold calling differ by an order of magnitude among public numbers: 3%–5% is a broad definition that includes multiple follow-up rounds and a half-warm list, and 0.45% is the strictest definition that counts only "sent to brand-new contacts." The difference isn't about who is lying, it's about the denominator [our judgment].
- The wording in Article 1033 of the Civil Code about "intruding upon another person's private life peace by means of telephone, text message, instant messaging tools, and the like" — this book could not verify reliable original text, so it is not used as an argument; if your legal team needs a civil-law basis, please check the original text.
- This chapter gives the nationwide minimum line. Regulated categories like healthcare, finance, law, insurance, and education have stricter industry rules of their own; check with your legal team once more before implementing.
