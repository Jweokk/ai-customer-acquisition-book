# Chapter 14 Content Others Cannot Copy: First-Party Data and Real Cases

Content used to be a contest of who wrote faster and laid down more keywords, and one piece took a person several days from topic to publication. Now the cost of generating a piece has become a single compute call, and output itself is no longer worth anything. What you have to do is turn the raw material in your hands into evidence nobody else can produce. Content in front of buyers has already flooded; what decides whom they pick has become the material "only you have." That is why this chapter gives you 5 methods to turn first-party data and real cases into a moat.

---

## Method 1: Turn the traces customers leave with you into publishable raw material

**What to do**: Gather the behavioral records customers generate themselves inside your systems (website, App, CRM, stores, customer service) into raw material that only you can obtain and that you can also cite externally.

**How**

1. First keep four things apart. What you observed inside your own business, with collection records, reproducible, is first-party data; aggregator sites, tool-vendor summaries, and self-media retellings are leads only and must go back to the original publisher; packaging secondhand statistics as "our research," or generating something that "looks like a number," is pseudo-first-party data; and a model restating your existing content is AI paraphrase, which cannot serve as evidence either. Only the first kind can go into public material.
2. Get the "recognize the person" step done first. Leads from the website, mini-programs, Douyin (Chinese: 抖音), and WeCom (Chinese: 企微) each carry an identifier, stitched together by a company name or a unified ID, so the system knows that "these records are the same person" — a CDP market guide judges that identity resolution is the dividing line between a real CDP and a white-label tool [third-party verified]. If identity resolution cannot be done, every later step of processing rests on a wrong base.
3. Choose the processing direction correctly: turn proprietary data into something useful to the buyer — selection comparisons, parameter benchmarks, industry averages. Taking it to advertising makes it inventory.
4. De-identify or anonymize before publishing externally, and remember that the two are not equal: de-identified data is still personal information and can still be re-identified, while only anonymization is irreversible. Have legal walk through it before you write.

**Example**: A company making industrial sewing machines and CNC machine tools used a "company name / unified ID" to stitch together isolated leads from the website, mini-programs, and Douyin, and reports that cross-channel manual entry work fell 34% and overseas leads-to-opportunity conversion rose 29% [vendor claim]. The industrial-goods platform ZKH (Chinese: 震坤行) built a product pool from data on tens of millions of products, tens of billions of transactions, plus user behavior data, and says that 11 months after launch it had recommended more than 20 million lines of materials to customers, adding more than 100 million yuan in revenue and serving more than 270 group customers [vendor claim].

**Pitfalls**: Mistaking a "list" for "leads." A list of 50,000 rows is 50,000 identifiers, not 50,000 leads — a list with no record of origin and no consent status can neither be cited externally nor used compliantly. Also, figures such as "first-party data activation can cut CAC by as much as 50% and lift revenue 10–15%" come from data providers' own research; treat them as direction only, not as a basis for budgeting [third-party verified].

## Method 2: Have customers fill in a preference instead of guessing

**What to do**: Where the customer is willing to trade information for a better recommendation, ask about preferences directly — a questionnaire, a preference center, a selection checklist, a purchase-intent form.

**How**

1. Keep "actively filled in" separate from "passively collected." Zero-party data means preference information the customer actively and voluntarily shares (Forrester's definition, retold by a zero-party-data tool vendor) [vendor claim]; it is worth more than the behavior you guess at in the back end, because the customer knows what he is giving and what he is getting. Asking directly, "do you care more about delivery speed or unit price?", is more useful than counting how many times he looked at the pricing page.
2. One question, one use. Three preferences filled in should buy a selection checklist the buyer can put to work immediately; if they buy only "we will give you a better experience," nobody fills it in next time.
3. Design how many data points you collect. A personalized journey rests on a dozen or so stable preference points per person, not on asking thirty questions at once and having nobody finish.
4. If you collect it, use it. Whatever he ticked, the later case studies, parameter pages, and follow-up emails should be ordered by it; collecting without using is the same as telling him the form was pointless.

**Example**: A survey cited in a strategy review shows that only 29% of consumers trust how brands use their data, 71% expect personalization, and 81% worry about how companies handle personal information; but when the process is transparent, willingness to provide information is 2.5 times higher, and 47% are willing to share when there is a clear benefit [third-party verified]. For comparison: ASICS collects 21.5 data points per user through a questionnaire to build a personalized journey, e.l.f. Cosmetics collects 11 preference points per customer, and Sephora uses a beauty quiz to make recommendations [third-party verified].

**Pitfalls**: Making the questionnaire a one-off campaign that asks a pile of questions without giving the corresponding benefit. What a customer hands over by voluntarily paying the cost of filling in a form is a signal source competitors are unwilling to match; if nothing comes back after submission, that cost was wasted.

## Method 3: Dig the numbers out of your own operations

**What to do**: Turn observations from the operating floor into content raw material — your delivery cycle times, the distribution of return reasons, equipment failure rates on different production lines, the ten questions customers ask most often in inquiries.

**How**

1. Start with the cheapest place: the questions sales and customer service actually answered, logged once a week and turned into topics. This is the lowest-cost entry point into data from your own operations.
2. Give the data its basis: how big the sample was, when it is from, how it was calculated. Without a clear basis, readers will assume you are making it up.
3. Write only the kind that exists nowhere else on the internet. Data others also have is a supplement; only the singular belongs in a flagship piece.
4. Product and integrations are exclusive data too. A tool product can inventory "who we integrate with"; a company going overseas can compare how the same set of pages performs across different markets and languages — an observation nobody else can obtain.

**Example**: From data on its own 6000+ apps, Zapier used templates to generate three tiers of landing pages, and its monthly organic search traffic rose from about 1.19 million to nearly 4.8 million between 2020-11 and 2023-11 [third-party verified]. Glasp stopped querying models externally and instead read its own server logs to see directly which pages the models were citing, growing ChatGPT-sourced traffic from 500 to 19,000 daily sessions [third-party verified]. On the China side, industrial-goods marketing driven by linked supply-chain data raised SQL conversion 32% [third-party verified]. There is a counterexample too: with 1.7 million+ programmatic pages covering 20 markets and 10 languages, Wise earned about 54 million monthly organic visits; while G2, held up as a SaaS benchmark, was reported to have lost about 80% of its organic traffic by the end of 2025 [third-party verified] — the fork is decided by whether there is proprietary, structured, continuously refreshed data behind the pages.

**Pitfalls**: Deciding your own data "isn't sexy" and not logging it. Things like delivery cycle times and return reasons do not sound like selling points, but they are the raw material that best supports "only I have this"; go looking for them when it is time to write and you will most likely no longer remember them.

## Method 4: Build cases into evidence with all four things in place

**What to do**: Build a case library in which every case must satisfy four hard requirements at once; miss one and it does not qualify.

**How**

1. **Named**: the real name of the company or institution, the date, and a verifiable event. Where naming is not possible, it must be explicitly labeled "**Composite profile | constructed from a common industry pattern, not a named event**," and "a certain company A / B / C" — fake specificity — is not allowed.
2. **Traceable**: the original link actually opens when tested, and the data timestamp is written down. Platform rules change by the month; a number with no timestamp is no number at all.
3. **Tiered**: each item states the nature of its source, with speculation listed separately; when several sources give different figures for the same thing, the differences are set side by side rather than picking the one that looks best.
4. **Bounded**: every positive case comes with a failure condition, a customer type it does not suit, or a counterexample. For a signal to be credible, the costs borne by senders must differ enough; a case that bears no cost is equivalent to no signal [official].

**Example**: The case "we took a client from 67 to 2100+ signups a month," presented on its own, has publicity value only [vendor claim]; attach its preconditions — that the product itself could be copied, that growth rested on a programmatic page matrix, and that this is exactly the shape Google named explicitly after March 2024 — and it becomes evidence that peers and buyers can jointly examine. For a counterexample, take the four criteria NewsGuard uses to track AI content farms (clear evidence that a site's content is largely AI-produced, published with no meaningful human oversight, presented in a way that makes ordinary readers think it came from human journalists, and not clearly disclosed), which its own site says have identified 3,749 sites across 16 languages [official]. Another kind of cost arrives faster: in April 2025, users of the AI code editor Cursor received a reply signed "Sam" saying that "one subscription can only cover one device" was a new security policy — the policy was pure fabrication, and Sam was a bot; users took it as an official change and publicly announced they were cancelling their subscriptions, and co-founder Michael Truell subsequently apologized, attributing the incident to hallucinations and non-deterministic output [third-party verified].

**Pitfalls**: Putting up only the successes and leaving out the preconditions. Fabricated "facts" cost money outright: the FTC's Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials was adopted on 2024-08-14, explicitly banning fake or fabricated consumer reviews, reviews exchanged for compensation with a particular slant, insider reviews without disclosure, and company-controlled review sites falsely claiming to be independent [official]. In April 2026, the FTC won a 4 million USD judgment against the supplement brand TruHeight and its principal, a case that included thousands of five-star reviews on its website actually written by employees, free or discounted products exchanged for positive reviews, and software-generated reviews posted through bot accounts disguised as real users [official]. In the same period the FTC ran five parallel cases under Operation AI Comply, and DoNotPay paid 193,000 USD to settle claims made without evidence [official].

## Method 5: Put a calendar behind the gathering, and grade on evidence only

**What to do**: Turn first-party data into a pipeline rather than a one-off project, and switch the grading from output to evidence.

**How**

1. Put four things on the calendar: a quarterly "own-data study" with method, sample, and basis; a monthly maintenance pass on a list of "facts we assert externally" (version, owner, change log), so that both people and AI read the correct version; a weekly log of customer questions; and a self-run experiment every six months to verify whether the output of the first three actually enters the buyer's decision process. The marginal cost of experiments can be pushed down — one landmark company does this by running more than 20,000 controlled experiments per team per year [official].
2. Change the KPIs. Demote "how many pieces published, how many keywords covered, how many reads"; promote the number of new proprietary data points per month, the increase in citations, the error rate in how AI describes you, and the pass rate of case-library links under testing. A single month's reading means nothing; use the same set of questions and watch the trend on a rolling basis — one third-party analysis notes that 40%–60% of the sources cited in AI answers are replaced month by month [third-party verified].
3. For material generated in bulk with AI, from September 2025 there is no "unlabeled" option: the Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic Content took effect on 2025-09-01, requiring AI-generated text, images, audio, and video to carry both explicit and implicit labels, with distribution platforms verifying the labels at the listing and publishing stage [official].
4. Do not mistake quantity for maturity — 50 experiments with no hypothesis are worth less than 10 aligned with strategic goals [vendor claim]. The same lesson holds when transferred to content.

| Old KPI (demoted) | Evidence KPI (promoted) |
|---|---|
| Content output (pieces/items) | New proprietary data points per month, cases filed into the library |
| Keywords covered | AI citation frequency, share of cited pages containing first-party data |
| Reads, impressions | Error rate in the facts AI restates about you |
| Publishing frequency | Share of cases meeting all four requirements, pass rate of links under testing |

**Example**: The same batch of content on two kinds of domain ends completely differently. The SEO platform SE Ranking first published 6 AI-assisted articles on its own authoritative, human-edited blog, for about 555,000 impressions and 2300+ clicks, 4 of which Google AI Overviews cited as sources; it then used "one-click generation, zero human involvement" to publish 2000 pure-AI articles across 20 brand-new domains — in the first 36 days, 70.95% of pages were indexed and 28% briefly entered the top 100, but after about 3 months the top-100 share fell from 28% to 3%, had still not recovered 16 months later, and totaled only 1381 clicks [third-party verified]. The policy side is that in March 2024 Google upgraded its old policy into "scaled content abuse" — content produced in bulk to manipulate rankings and of very low value to users is treated as spam regardless of whether it is generated by automation, humans, or a combination; Google says actual removals reached 45% after the update [official]. The direction has already carried named costs in the content industry: Stereogum's founder wrote in November 2025 that Google's shift toward AI search cut their ad revenue by 70% [official]; and Chegg admitted in May 2023 that ChatGPT was affecting its new-customer growth, with its share price closing down 48.41% the next day [third-party verified].

**Pitfalls**: Assuming "publishing a lot" is a competitive advantage, or conversely that AI content is bound to fall. Both are wrong: what gets cleaned out is the combination "brand-new domain + zero human involvement + pure AI"; content on an authoritative domain with a record of human verification kept getting traffic and AI citations. There is one red line only — scaled production to manipulate rankings.

## Notes for this chapter

- This chapter does not cite conclusions such as "AI content is bound to be de-ranked." The same content gets AI citations on an authoritative, human-edited domain and survives about three months on a brand-new, unedited one — the difference lies in the two variables "domain authority + human involvement," not in whether AI was used. [third-party verified]
- The buyer's position can be stated through that 1970 paper on the "lemons" market: unable to tell real from fake, he can only price all content at average credibility, and that average is being pulled down — the people putting out inferior goods squeeze out the whole market, the rule-following businesses along with it. [official]
- Industry differences are folded into the methods: the case libraries and risk lists of B2B and industrial businesses function, in practice, as a one-time alignment carried out on the buyer's behalf inside their own organization — a Gartner 2025 survey says 74% of B2B buying teams show "unhealthy conflict" during the decision process, while buying groups that reach consensus are 2.5 times more likely to report a "high-quality deal" than those that do not [vendor claim]; the cheapest first-party asset of a tool product is in-product behavior and integration data; regulated industries such as education and finance can publish the least, so their first-party assets go first to improving decisions internally, and externally they release only de-identified aggregate conclusions confirmed by legal. [our judgment]
- For the China-side points on "AI poisoning" (feeding large models with fake reviews in bulk) and the Self-Discipline Convention for the Generative Engine Optimization Industry, the draft found only vendor press releases quoting them, and could not verify the original CCTV broadcast material or the list of convention signatories, so this chapter does not cite them and gives no figures.
- The point that "disclosing the use of AI lowers engagement" appears only as media retelling, and the authors and year written in the Journal of Consumer Research study cited were not checked against the original journal, so it serves as direction only. A brand making "no AI" a selling point (for example Aerie re-pushing "100% Aerie Real" in spring 2026, with quarterly sales up 23% year on year) is an isolated phenomenon in a high-trust category and cannot be extrapolated. [third-party verified]
- Compliance standards are evolving fast: the rules on automated decision-making and outbound data transfers in personal information protection, the FTC's enforcement posture on the review rule, and the implementation details of the AI content labeling measures are all changing. Before putting anything into practice, go by the current regulatory text. [official]
