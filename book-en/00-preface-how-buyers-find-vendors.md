# Preface · How Buyers Find Vendors Has Changed

Buyers no longer pick vendors only in the search box and at trade shows — they ask an AI, they let an agent do the filtering, they look at what platforms push and at what other people say. This book teaches you how to win customers in these new places, and in the old ones too.

Those two sentences are the only thing in this book you need to memorize. Every method in every chapter that follows is what they look like taken apart.

## What Changed Is the Step Where They Pick

First, be clear about what changed. "A vendor gets picked" has moved four times in a little over thirty years.

The portal era (late 1990s to early 2000s): the gateway was a hand-maintained directory — Yahoo in the United States; Sina (Chinese: 新浪), Sohu (Chinese: 搜狐) and hao123 in China. The job a company had to do was called "getting listed"; the power sat with editors, and whether you made the directory was visible at a glance.

The search era (mid-2000s to the 2010s): the gateway became keywords. Google and Baidu (Chinese: 百度) handed the power of filtering to ranking algorithms, and companies held two levers for the first time — optimize organic rankings, or buy keyword positions outright. Traffic now had a public price, and the customer acquisition budget became a visible cost line on the books. The division into content teams, SEO teams and paid-media teams all grew out of this assembly line.

The recommendation-feed era (around 2016 to today): Douyin (Chinese: 抖音) and Toutiao (Chinese: 今日头条) flipped the gateway over — users used to look for content; now content looks for users. Companies responded by going all-in on content, and short-video teams and livestream rooms grew up alongside the paid-media teams. This stage buried one thing — traffic allocation became a black box for the first time: you roughly know the rules (completion rate, engagement), but you don't know why any single allocation happened.

The AI and agent era (late 2022 to today): the gateway folded from a column of results into one answer. On November 30, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT [official]. In May 2024, at its I/O conference, Google named its generative search experience AI Overviews and launched it in the United States [official]. For the first time, a buyer can outsource the whole thing — "find me a few vendors that do X, and compare whether A or B suits my budget better" — to a model.

This time there is a substantive difference from the previous three: in those, your position was "what rank"; in this one, your position is "present or not". The losers aren't moved to page two — they don't exist.

We tested that conclusion ourselves before starting this book. Using 6 real procurement questions (3 Chinese–English pairs, covering three categories — CRM, MES and study-abroad agencies), we asked each question twice on each of 5 model channels. Asking the same question of the same model twice in a row, the overlap of the recommended lists was only 22%–56%; asking a different model, the pairwise overlap was only 4%–21%, and merging the lists from several models produced more than 40 names. Of 51 valid answers, those with clickable sources: 0 [our own measurement].

One boundary has to be marked on that last point: "AI answers carry citations" belongs to the product layer — a search product with web access attaches sources to its answers, and this is invisible at the model layer. Tell which layer a sentence is speaking about before deciding whether it can be cited — this discipline is used throughout the book.

## One Formula

Taken apart, customer acquisition is one multiplication:

> Acquisition result = number of touchpoints × relevance × trust × conversion rate ÷ cost

The four factors describe four different things. Number of touchpoints is in how many places the same person can run into you; relevance is whether, at the moment he runs into you, what you're saying is exactly what he's looking for; trust is whether he believes you; conversion rate is whether, once he believes you, he's willing to talk and willing to sign. Cost sits in the denominator — it's the money the first terms burn between them, and it doesn't care where it goes: compute, data and sources are all line items newly added this round. This book doesn't recognize "free traffic" as a thing, only "traffic whose cost hasn't been priced yet".

AI made the first two cheaper. The marginal cost of producing content falls, so one deep piece can be split into dozens of items spread across several gateways, and the number of touchpoints goes from "how much the budget can buy" to "how much the capacity can put out"; relevance got cheaper too — machines will read your website, your prices and your specs, as long as you write the facts in a form they can read and check. These two used to rest on craft; now they look more and more like configuration.

The third, by contrast, became scarcer. When everyone's content can be mass-produced, what the buyer sees at the AI has become how other people rate you, whether you put the risk up front, whether you have first-party data and real cases — and what you say about yourself drops down the order. Numbers can be synthesized; "I've actually used this thing" cannot. So the first two getting cheaper doesn't mean acquisition gets cheaper — with more touchpoints and higher relevance, and trust not moving along with them, the same budget only buys you more people who "have seen it".

On the buyer's side there is one more thing you will never see. The dark funnel has always existed — the term "dark social" appeared as early as 2012 [third-party verified], meaning the sharing and discussion that leaves no source behind: a line at the dinner table, "which one do you all use", a line in a private group chat, "send me the quote". Once AI arrived, this stretch of the decision left a log for the first time — what he asked, who he ruled out, who he compared you against. Only that log sits with the buyer and the model vendors, and is not shown to the seller. This asymmetry matters far more than "organic traffic is shrinking".

## How to Read This Book

The order has three steps.

First, use AI to make the old methods fast and accurate. None of the old roads have disappeared; the way you walk them has changed. Advertising, outreach, in-platform acquisition — you're already doing these, and handing the repetitive labor inside them to AI shows results the same day.

Then let an agent string the actions together. Building lists, publishing content, taking inquiries, following up leads — these tasks repeat over and over, and only once they're chained do you get to talk about scale.

Finally, position yourself at the new gateways. Getting AI to mention you in its answers, squeezing into an agent's candidate set, cultivating the evidence third parties hold — all three wait on your facts, cases and data being ready first; otherwise you can hold the seat and still not catch what comes to it.

If you're in a hurry, jump around using the table above, and read first the chapter that matches the step you're on right now.

The book treats the Chinese market and overseas markets side by side. The credibility mechanisms of Doubao (Chinese: 豆包), DeepSeek, Kimi and Tencent Yuanbao (Chinese: 腾讯元宝) differ from those of ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google AI Overviews, and the source structures of Xiaohongshu (Chinese: 小红书), Douyin, the WeChat (Chinese: 微信) ecosystem and the open web differ too. What transfers across markets is mechanisms and principles; channels and tools have to be redone, and compliance has to be checked once on each side.

On numbers: every figure carries a source tier at the end of its sentence — [official], [third-party verified], [vendor claim], [our judgment], [our own measurement]. Anything marked [our own measurement] comes with its method, sample, time and boundaries, so you can reproduce it yourself, or falsify it yourself.

## Where This Book Stops

This book stops at the first completed transaction, at the signing of a contract. Retention, repeat purchase, customer success, cross-selling and upsell are out of scope — that is the subject of *AI Customer Lifecycle Management* (Chinese: 《AI 客户经营》). The reason is simple: the qualification for acquisition is settled at the front end — whether you got into the buyer's decision process is basically fixed before sales says its first word.

## Notes

- The eras in the preface serve only to orient you. The start and end times of the four gateway shifts are a narrative frame that helps people understand; the eras overlap, and this is not precise periodization.
- Several widely circulated industry figures (a short-video platform's daily active user scale, the user scale and penetration rate of generative AI, the amount involved in a deepfaked video-conference fraud) currently cannot be traced to an original publisher's source, so they are not used in the body at all.
- The candidate-set figures come from one round of the book's own measurement (6 questions, 3 Chinese–English pairs, 5 model channels, each asked twice). They describe behavior at the model memory layer, and cannot be extrapolated to the citation behavior of consumer AI search products.
- "AI answers carry citations" belongs to the product layer and is invisible at the model layer. First tell whether a sentence is speaking about the product layer or the model layer, then decide whether it can be cited.
