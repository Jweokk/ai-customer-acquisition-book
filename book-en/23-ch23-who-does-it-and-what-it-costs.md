# Chapter 23 Who Does It and What It Costs: Three Minimum Configurations

When AI projects stalled in the past, people tended to blame the model: not a new enough version, not big enough parameters, prompts not written well. Now the tools are on the table and the capability is sufficient; what blocks AI customer acquisition is almost always the organization — who does it, whose money pays for it, what goes to machines, and what must stay with people. What you have to do is: sort the work into buckets first, then staff it and track every dollar by company size.

Every method above ultimately comes down to “who is doing it”. So this chapter gives you 5 methods to allocate people and money by company size.

---

## Method 1: Decide up front which work machines may not touch

**What to do**: Use three criteria to sort the process into buckets, then write the “things machines may not touch” into a one-page boundary.

**How**

1. Hand work to a machine only if all three criteria are met: repetitive (how many times a week or a month it happens, and whether the pattern is stable); information-dense (how much context must be read and how many systems must be checked to do it correctly); and reversible (can an error be caught, can it be undone, is the cost controllable). The third is the core one, and it is a business property, not a technical one: an outbound email cannot be recalled once sent, and a quote in the buyer's hands constitutes a commitment.
2. By the criteria, drop every step of the process into one of three buckets: steps expressible as if-then rules get ordinary code — if plain code can solve it, don't use a model; steps that have thousands of human judgment records with outcomes and low-risk output forms (yes/no, routing, labeling) go to a large model with full measurement; steps that are high-risk or short on samples go to a human in the loop, with an agent assembling the evidence and a person making the decision in 30 seconds. After bucketing, people move from execution down to review — no fewer people, a different position.
3. Put the escalation threshold on “emotion and failure-risk signals”, not on “problem type”. Routing by type (pre-sales to AI, after-sales to humans) will miss the most expensive class of customers: the one who is already unhappy but has not yet decided whether to leave.
4. Write a one-page usage boundary, with six rules locked down: externally facing facts may come only from the fact list, and anything with no entry cannot be said by a machine; questions of price, qualifications, and commitments must be signed off by a person; emotion and failure-risk signals trigger escalation to a human; every automated action is reversible, leaves a trace, and has a circuit breaker, with the stop conditions written at procurement time; any statement involving “research shows” or “data shows” must be able to name the original publisher, and anything you cannot verify gets deleted; if employees are using unapproved tools, the approved tools have a gap, and the right move is to speed up delivery of a governable platform.
5. Delete “equivalent to N people” from your reports and replace it with three layered metrics: automatic resolution rate for routine questions, escalation rate for emotion and failure-risk signals, and fact-statement rollback rate (the number of times a machine got it wrong).

**Example**: Klarna's official press release said its AI assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, work equivalent to 700 full-time agents [vendor claim]; in 2025 the CEO acknowledged that an organization built around cost first “ends up delivering lower quality” and announced it was hiring human agents again [third-party reported]; by 2026 the workload carried by AI had grown to roughly the equivalent of 850 full-time agents, alongside a rebuilt in-house team of about 100 highly skilled staff [third-party reported]. Stack the three together and you get a certainty-and-agent bucket expanding while the human-in-the-loop bucket shrinks but refines.

**Pitfalls**: Treating “AI and frustrated customers don't get along” as the model not being strong enough. A field experiment by Dartmouth's Tuck School of Business based on Alibaba's customer service found that AI shortened routine inquiries but struggled in sessions with high emotion and high service-failure risk, even with human involvement [third-party research reported] — this is a threshold-design problem. Nor should you put “equivalent to N people” into your own reports: it blends volume handled, complexity distribution, the quality definition, and cost shifting into a single number.

## Method 2: Turn the acquisition budget into asset purchases

**What to do**: Turn the budget table from three lines (media, agency fees, labor) into four asset line items, then add the two lines of cost most often left out.

**How**

1. Give each of the four asset types its own line item: the fact foundation (every fact has a version, an owner, and a change flow; pages are structured, documents are machine-readable, and the customer-service and sales AI knowledge bases hang off the same fact source); first-hand evidence (data you measured yourself, retellable customer cases, verifiable controlled experiments — one investment, many rounds of reuse); third-party sources (what you buy is “the name of the source when it is cited”); and reversible automation and measurement (the steps that land in the agent bucket, plus the cost of building the three layers of evidence).
2. Add the two overlooked cost lines: compute and tokens — this is sliding from the IT line to the acquisition line, and mass generation plus mass outreach make token cost rise along with lead volume; and the hidden labor of human-machine collaboration — teams that adopt digital employees still spend time feeding context, checking output, debugging, rerunning prompts, and cleaning up “confident but wrong” answers, about 6.4 hours a week, and those 6.4 hours are not an efficiency dividend but a new operating cost [third-party reported] (the sample size of that survey could not be traced to the original report page this round; this chapter quotes only the duration — see the notes for this chapter).
3. Do not set up an “AI acquisition project”. The Salesforce CEO has made a public judgment: if there is a standalone “AI budget”, you are doing AI the wrong way [third-party reported]. The typical ending of a project budget is that the year ends, the project closes, and the assets go to zero; to judge whether a budget has migrated, ask one question only — what share of the money will leave something behind when this quarter ends [our judgment].
4. Testing ammunition must be a fixed line in the budget table, not what is left at year-end.
5. Tie tool budget to process change: for every tool expense, write down “which step of the process changes, who changes it, and what number you watch after the change”; if you cannot write it down, don't buy yet. Prioritize the workflow with the most handoffs, not the one with the largest volume — time hides in queues and handoffs.
6. Treat shadow AI as a diagnostic signal, not a violation: 70%–80% of employees use unapproved tools, and inside one chip company 1,500–1,600 tools were in use at the same time [third-party reported]. When employees use unapproved tools, the approved tools have a product gap.

**Example**: A 2026 marketing budget allocation guide offers this structural reference: digital channels 40%–50%, content and brand 20%–30%, events and sponsorship 10%–15%, research and contingency 10%–20%, with a fixed 8%–12% set aside for testing new channels, new audiences, and new tactics [vendor claim]. The counterexample comes from a Chinese-side first-person account: the company gave every department AI tools and ran dozens of training sessions, and the year-end review showed actual AI usage under 15% [third-party reported]; overseas, the same phenomenon has the structure of 5%–10% heavy users, 20% occasional users who use it crudely, and 70% who barely use it at all [third-party reported].

**Pitfalls**: Evaluating tools by satisfaction and the number of users. The UK Department for Business and Trade's pilot of M365 Copilot (1,000 seats, 3 months) showed only 1.14 actions per person per day, PowerPoint 7 minutes faster but half the quality, and Excel slower and worse, with the self-assessment concluding that it “found no robust evidence that time savings translated into productivity gains” — yet 72% of users reported satisfaction [third-party reported]. Satisfaction decoupled from output is exactly why shelfware can stay hidden for three years.

## Method 3: Staff and fund by company size — three tiers listing only what fails without it

**What to do**: Find your own seat and work out the minimum you cannot do without at your tier.

**How**

1. **1–2-person companies** (micro team, single category): the only thing to do at this tier is start accumulating assets; do not touch automation. Fix a question set of 20–30 real buyer questions, run an entry-point audit monthly (fixed question set → fixed entry points and frequency → record only four fields each time → watch the trend, not a single run); build a fact list v0, starting with 20 entries, each with one owner, one version number, and one effective date, and the owner is the boss; no agents, no outbound automation, no mass content. Spend the money on running the question set and maintaining the fact list, not on tools. The test: three months later, can you state “what our stability rate is, and which facts were stated wrongly”.
2. **5–15 people** (small to midsize team, single market): this tier has to put human-machine division of labor in place and start having dedicated roles. One full-stack growth engineer (can be grown internally), fully accountable for “whether the buyer is described correctly”; one to two content and source operators, whose assessment switches to evidence KPIs (number of first-hand materials filed, number of cases with all four required elements, consistency rate of third-party statements); one AI visibility owner (concurrent role), tied to the fact-list owner; use the three criteria from Method 1 to bucket the first three steps of the acquisition process, landing no more than 2–3 agents plus 2 human checkpoints. Spend the money on people and process change, with tool spending tied to the process. The test: at least one process achieves “people demoted from execution to review” without the failure rate rising.
3. **50+ people** (multiple categories, multiple markets): the core at this tier is cross-functional authority. Form a cross-functional squad — full-stack growth engineer, content and sources, data, legal/compliance liaison — reporting to someone who can command marketing, sales, and product at the same time, otherwise the process redesign will not move; one question set per language per market; visibility written into the assessment of one business-line owner, never as “jointly owned across departments”; appoint an STO (single-threaded owner), one unique owner per important thing. Spend the money on setting up the cross-functional squad's authority and on the bilingual question sets and fact list. The test: if there is a single instance of “nobody can say who owns this”, this tier is not yet standing.

**Example**: Someone has priced a “fully staffed” growth team: a few engineers plus 1 PM and 1 designer, about $1 million a year; the rule of thumb for carrying that cost is an ARR of at least $5 million [third-party verified: first-hand from a practitioner].

**Pitfalls**: Treating the three tiers as a ladder and assuming that with more people you should naturally move up. The three tiers list only the moves that fail without; everything beyond them can be postponed. The way the vast majority of companies stumble at this step is remarkably consistent: the tools are in place, the process is unchanged; the process changes, the assessment is unchanged; the assessment changes, the people are not.

## Method 4: Assign three things to one named person each

**What to do**: Assign one owner each to three new roles and to the question of “how AI retells us”.

**How**

1. The full-stack growth engineer. The old division of labor was an assembly line — the content team makes content, the SEO team does rankings, the paid team buys traffic, SDRs make calls — and each role owns one segment, with nobody accountable for “whether the buyer was described wrongly”. This role has to touch pages, data, structured markup, and experiments at the same time; its value is making one person fully accountable for “whether the buyer is described correctly”. The pricing signal from the market side is already out: FDE (forward-deployed engineer) hiring grew more than 1,000% year over year, with total compensation of $300,000–$550,000 for mid-to-senior levels, and the most valued skills have shifted to customer discovery, problem decomposition, and AI product judgment [third-party reported]. The industry's shared conclusion about this kind of role is an anti-hiring line: “You can't hire an FDE, you can only grow one.”
2. The content and source operator. The object of work shifts from “content output volume” to: digging up and filing first-hand materials, whether the four required elements of the case library are complete, whether statements in third-party sources match reality, and whether community answers conflict with the fact list. The tightest thing for this role is consistency of outward statements — when the fact list changes, what must change with it is usually not just the website but also structured markup, third-party platform profiles, sales scripts and quote templates, the customer-service and sales AI knowledge bases, and archived historical content. There is no technical substitute for this; it needs one clear owner.
3. Ownership of AI visibility. Don't argue about “which department”; give it to whoever “owns the outward facts” — the position where the fact-list owner sits. Visibility's downstream is citation and retelling, its upstream is facts and structure, and the middle is third-party sources; the true owner is the common owner of these three things. The owner must be a person, not a department — writing “marketing department” actually means nobody.
4. Every volume target must carry a quality guardrail metric; every important thing gets a unique owner with enough authority and decision rights; each business pillar names 1–2 full-time champions who spend 50%–100% of their time on process change. The champion must be dedicated — a part-time champion is no champion at all.

**Example**: In the Chinese ecosystem the same evolution is stated more bluntly: Anker Innovations (Chinese: 安克创新) proposed that by 2027 it will essentially eliminate segmented roles such as front-end and QA, keeping only three roles — business engineer (full stack), AI engineer, and product manager [third-party reported]; another organization treats the “end-to-end owner” as the smallest unit of reform [third-party reported]. The AI-native organization playbook from Meta's ads team gives two handles, the STO and the Agent Champion [third-party reported]; a RevOps playbook requires every volume target to carry a quality guardrail metric, on the grounds that benchmarking showed a high rate of reward hacking [vendor claim].

**Pitfalls**: Reading “full stack” as “cheap labor that does everything”. Once KPIs slice it into “how many posts you published” and “how many pages you built”, the role degenerates into the previous era's generalist, and all you save is headcount.

## Method 5: Change the hiring filter and the review yardstick — move one lever per quarter

**What to do**: Settle three things in one pass: whom to hire, what to assess, and at what pace to change.

**How**

1. On the hiring side, change the filter. The old filter was a skills checklist; the new one is learnability and verification ability. Two checkable signals: can the candidate design an evaluation loop (eval, regarded in the industry as the strongest signal of real large-model experience); and does the candidate have “fabrication literacy”, able to state clearly within 30 seconds which model they are using and what it costs [third-party reported].
2. Guard against one side effect: a hollowed-out junior pipeline. Redefine entry-level roles as “verification roles” (fact spot-checks, hallucination checks, spec writing) rather than simply freezing headcount — otherwise in three to five years there will be a gap in senior acquisition people who understand the business.
3. On the assessment side, change the yardstick. Content output volume becomes the number of first-hand materials filed and the cleanliness of the fact list; ranking position becomes stability rate; lead count becomes the count of valid leads after five-tier segmentation and the lead lift verified by incremental experiments; reach becomes reply rate plus quality guardrail metrics (unsubscribe rate, complaint rate).
4. Move only one lever per quarter, in this recommended order: Q1 the fact foundation (fact list v0 plus structured-content work, no process change, no tools bought); Q2 human-machine division of labor (bucket by the three criteria, land 2–3 agents plus 2 human checkpoints); Q3 sources and external evidence; Q4 measurement and assessment. Moving one at a time is non-negotiable.
5. Set the budget for continuous reinforcement, not for a one-time push. The shape of AI tools changes every month, and the finish line keeps moving.

**Example**: Indeed data shows overall hiring is weak (postings are only about 6% above the February 2020 baseline), but postings mentioning AI are more than 130% above baseline and make up 4.2% of U.S. postings, with the AI mention rate in marketing roles jumping from 8.4% to 14.9% [third-party reported]. On the other side is the failure rate of change: a Gartner survey shows that only about 32% of mid- and senior-level business leaders believe their most recent change achieved “healthy change adoption”, and 79% of employees have low trust in change, while “routinizing” change raises the probability of healthy adoption on time by about 3 times [official claim].

**Pitfalls**: Talking about AI transformation without changing assessment is forcing employees to pretend to use AI. And don't turn assessment into another kind of pretending — the output of someone who truly knows how to use AI in 3 hours a day can exceed 10 hours from someone who does not; assess by hours worked and it is the productive one who loses.

## Notes for this chapter

- Attributing AI implementation failure to “the model not being strong enough” is rejected by all three research efforts: BCG surveyed 59 countries and 1,000 CxOs and found that only 26% of companies got value from AI beyond proof of concept, with about 70% of obstacles coming from people and process and only 10% related to the algorithm itself [official claim]; a Stanford study covering 51 real deployments concluded that in 77% of cases the hardest challenges were invisible (change management, data quality, and process reengineering) [third-party reported]; and MIT NANDA's report is widely retold as 95% of enterprise AI pilots producing no measurable return [third-party reported]. All three are self-reported or survey findings; agreement in direction is enough.
- Three items to be verified serve only as directional evidence: MIT NANDA's “95%/5%” could not be traced to the original report page or sample definition, and it measures “whether an AI pilot produced a fast P&L impact within 6 months”, which is often misread as a project failure rate; Glean's “6.4 hours a week of botsitting” comes from a research institute the vendor set up; the sample window and de-duplication definition behind the FDE hiring figure “+1,000% year over year” are not public. The Uber CTO's “burned through a whole year's token budget” comes through a third-party retelling and is used only as an order of magnitude.
- The three minimum configurations and the assessment-metric switchover table are [our judgment]; they are design values, not benchmarks. We suggest trial-running them for one quarter at one or two companies, then backfilling the thresholds once you have actual numbers for action volume and stability rate.
- On the Chinese side there are public disclosures of the same kind: a bank claims AI efficiency saved 15.56 million labor hours, equivalent to the workload of 8,000 employees, and a logistics company uses 28 “digital employees” to take on standardized work (both unnamed internal readings) [third-party reported]. Read numbers like these with the same question list: by what definition is the equivalent headcount converted? Is the equivalence in volume handled or in outcomes handled? Where did the released labor go?
- The ordering under “move only one lever per quarter” is our recommendation; it is the discipline of “one at a time” itself that this chapter is most willing to stand behind.
